My work asks one hard question: how does a robot keep track of what it cannot see? When a person walks behind a truck or a boulder, most trackers lose them and hand out a new identity when they reappear. I measure that failure and fix it, first in simulation and now on real driving data.

Part 1 · Simulation · Artemis lunar surface

Lunar Occlusion Tracking

A rover's mast camera tracks astronauts walking among boulders at the lunar south pole. When someone steps behind a rock or into a long polar shadow, does the tracker keep their identity, or call them a new person when they reappear?

I modelled 3-D line of sight to each person and built it into the tracker. A missed detection now counts as evidence that the person left only if the tracker expected to see them. From there the tracker climbs a ladder, one idea at a time, and every step is measured on identical detections.

What the experiments showed

  • More sensors make a naive tracker worse. With camera, lidar and a second camera it goes from 75.6 to 254 identity switches, because every sensor that cannot see a person reports them as gone. Sensors only help a tracker that knows what each one can see.
  • A second viewpoint beats a better sensor at the same spot. A second camera cut switches from 47.8 to 19.6. A lidar on the same mast cut them to 37.9.
  • Long hides need appearance. Motion alone keeps 7% of identities through hides over 5 s. Appearance re-identification lifts that to 50%, and a second camera to 87%.
  • An event camera works if the tracker reasons about visibility. I fitted an event camera model to real recordings (the EVOS dataset). With occlusion reasoning it matches the ordinary camera while people keep moving (51.4 against 47.8 switches, intervals overlap). It fails when people stand still, which told me the motion model needs a "stopped" mode.
  • Lidar masks from range alone. Instance segmentation on the lidar range image reaches 0.94 mean mask IoU against the true person masks.

Checked against industry tools

I ran MathWorks' built-in trackers on the same detections. Unaided, none of them keeps an identity through a hide. Given my visibility model, all three improve.

MATLAB, 10 seeds × 300 s. JIPDA with visibility beat my own motion-only tracker (33 against 44 switches per run). I report that because it showed me what to build next: soft association, which weighs every nearby detection instead of committing to one.

Limits. Simulated walkers and a sensor model, not a neural detector on real images. That is why Part 2 exists.

Part 2 · Real data · NVIDIA PhysicalAI-AV dataset

Occlusion Mining on Real Driving Data

NVIDIA's dataset has 306,152 real driving clips with 3-D boxes, but nothing in it says "this person is hidden right now". I wrote a line-of-sight test to find those moments: 16 rays from the front camera to each person, checked against every other object's 3-D box and, where lidar is available, against the nearest measured surface.

What the hides look like

  • Hides occur in 644 of 1,256 clips.
  • 3,858 end with the person back in view. 731 of those last 2 s or more. These are the test cases for a tracker's memory.
  • Cars do 57% of the hiding and other people 35%.

What I learned about the data

  • Person tracks in the machine labels are short. The typical one lasts 3.0 s of a 20 s clip, and 31% last under 1 s. Tracks never pause: a hidden person's box is guessed through the hide, or the track ends and a new ID starts.
  • The lidar is raw. Every point is recorded at its own instant, so I move each one with the car's motion before it lines up with the camera. I checked the result with a number: point angles match the beam angles to 0.03° at 8 m/s, and camera and lidar clocks agree to about 10 ms.
  • Boxes alone are a poor judge of visibility. On four clips, boxes call a person hidden in 41% of frames and the lidar in 32%. The lidar confirms only 56% of the box calls, because walls, poles and unlabelled parked cars have no box.
  • Small downloads from a 133 TB dataset. I pull one clip out of a remote archive with HTTP range requests: 10 MB instead of 2 GB.

A number I did not publish. My first count of identity switches on real data linked old and new IDs by motion alone. I hand-checked 10 of those links: 3 were plausible, 2 had linked a neighbour in a crowd, and 5 could not be judged from the image crops. So that result stays provisional until appearance, from segmentation masks, can settle who is who.

The dataset is gated under NVIDIA's licence, so this page shows aggregate numbers and no footage. The machine labels are not hand-checked. Code is available on request.

One person and one laptop, so the checks are built into the work.

  • It did not work the first time. The first tracker kept 5 to 9% of identities. I traced that to three separate causes: false tracks confirmed from clutter, hidden tracks hijacked by passers-by, and estimates drifting out of the blind zone. I fixed each with the standard remedy and measured each step.
  • Controlled comparisons. Every tracker sees identical detections and differs in one thing, so a result can be attributed to it.
  • Honest about limits. Each project states what it does not claim. Overlapping confidence intervals are reported as overlapping.
  • AI coding agents, held to evidence. I work with AI coding agents every day. They write quickly. I decide what to build, what counts as proof and what gets thrown away. Nothing reaches this page without a test, a second implementation or a hand check behind it.

Where this is going

The goal: track and segment every object in every frame, and keep each identity through every hide.

  1. My tracker on real boxes. Run it on the 3,858 hide-and-reveal events and report identity kept by hide duration, with IDF1 and HOTA.
  2. Identity from appearance. Use segmentation masks from SAM 3 on a GPU, so that who is who after a hide is settled by what the person looks like and not by where they might have walked.
  3. Close the two gaps the experiments exposed. Soft association in the style of JIPDA, and a motion model that knows people stop.
  4. Ground truth through hides. Masks, identities and measured visibility for every frame, so any tracker can be scored on exactly the moments that break it.

Open-Vocabulary Segmentation & Tracking

Text-prompted segmentation and tracking (MOTS) on KITTI and BDD100K driving video with Meta's SAM 3, merging per-prompt masklets into one identity space. Patched to run on CPU and Apple Silicon as well as CUDA.

Benchmarked SAM 3 against SAM 3.1 on KITTI-MOTS and MOTS20 with HOTA, on an A100 GPU. SAM 3 kept identity better on every benchmark: MOTS20 IDF1 84.8 vs 68.3, HOTA 72.6 vs 61.5. SAM 3.1 split one person into several tracks: 300 identities for 228 real pedestrians.

NeRF 3-D Reconstruction

A from-scratch PyTorch reimplementation of Neural Radiance Fields (Mildenhall et al., 2020): ray generation, positional encoding, coarse-to-fine sampling and volume rendering, with 29 tests.
